There is a claim going around engineering leadership right now, and I have heard some version of it from three different companies this year: every engineer should be talking to stakeholders and thinking like a product person, because agentic coding is going to make coding obsolete. It lands differently depending on who hears it. Some engineers nod. Some hear that their job is now a different job. I am somewhere in the middle, because the claim has two premises and a conclusion, and I think one premise is mostly right, one is mostly wrong, and the conclusion is right for reasons its advocates usually do not give and wrong in the word "every." This post is my attempt to check each piece against what is actually measured.

<!--more-->

Same rules as my [last post](/tech/future-of-software-engineering/): I separate what people with something to sell are saying, what the data shows, and what I believe. Where a number comes from a vendor, a recruiter, or a self-selected survey, I say so. Every figure has a footnote.

## Premise one: coding is becoming obsolete

The short version, since I spent five thousand words on it last week: the verb is wrong and the direction is right.

Human-typed code is heading toward a minority share at any company paying attention. Google says 75% of new code is AI-generated and engineer-approved, Anthropic says over 80% of its merged production code was authored by Claude in May, and Karpathy, who called coding agents slop in October 2025, said by March 2026 he had not typed code by hand since December.[^google75][^anthropic80][^karpathy] Those numbers are self-reported and lack denominators, but the direction is consistent.

What the same data does not say is that the engineering work went away. The largest field study I know of (802 developers, 196,000 pull requests, one company with a 2x output mandate) found merged PRs doubled, reviewer load doubled, and automated review overtook human review in volume. The paper is titled "AI Writes Faster Than Humans Can Review."[^vasilescu] GitClear has code duplication up 81% and refactoring down from 21% of changes to under 4%.[^gitclear] Veracode has 44% of raw model output introducing an OWASP Top 10 vulnerability, essentially unchanged from the prior year even as syntax correctness went to nearly 100%.[^veracode] Anthropic's own June report says code volume overstates productivity and human review is the bottleneck.[^anthropic80]

So "coding is obsolete" is the wrong frame. "Typing is cheap and checking is not" is the right one. What is actually becoming obsolete is a specific job: the person who receives a spec and translates it into code without owning either the spec or the consequences. That job is disappearing the way "computer programmer" disappeared as a BLS category between 2000 and 2015, down 45% while "software developer" nearly doubled.[^bls] The occupation defined by translation shrank. The occupation defined by ownership grew.

The premise is right about that. It is wrong if it means the engineering discipline goes away, because every dataset above says the discipline is now more necessary, not less, and it is concentrated in judgment: what to build, whether the thing built is correct, and who is accountable when it is not at 3am.

## Premise two: therefore engineers should do product work

Here the evidence is stronger than I expected, and it is not coming from product people.

DORA's 2025 report, about 5,000 respondents, identified seven capabilities that amplify the benefit of AI adoption. One of them is "user-centric focus," and DORA's finding is not that it helps. It is that adopting AI tools can actively harm teams that lack it. Their phrasing: speed is irrelevant if you are moving in the wrong direction.[^dora-caps] That is a correlational survey finding, not a controlled result, but it is the largest dataset on the question and it lands exactly where the claim's intuition lands. If output goes up 2x and nobody on the team is close to the user, you ship the wrong thing twice as fast.

Marty Cagan, who has been arguing for product-minded engineers for two decades and has an obvious interest in the conclusion, put the shift plainly in a podcast this month: for as long as he has been in the industry, CTOs said the bottleneck was that building takes a long time, so the focus was on engineers building. Now the bottleneck is "what do you want these people to build," which is the product question. He also said something less convenient for the product side, which I will come back to: AI has made it easier than ever to run the bad model, and he sees more garbage shipped now than at any point in his career.[^cagan]

The inside-the-lab view matches. Amol Avasare, Anthropic's head of growth, said in April that engineers are seeing 2 to 3x gains from Claude Code while team sizes stay flat, so "PM and design is just squeezed." Anthropic's rule: any project needing two weeks or less of engineering time has the engineer act as its PM, including talking to legal and cross-functional stakeholders.[^avasare] PostHog has run the whole company this way since 2020, with product engineers who talk to users, own the roadmap, do support, and are accountable for revenue and retention; their argument is that talking to users is like automated tests, a short-term cost that buys long-term speed.[^posthog]

The labor data has already moved. SignalFire has hiring at the twelve largest tech companies down 25% versus 2019, but engineering down only 11%, while product management is down 39% and design down 48%. Engineers were 55% of all hires in 2025.[^signalfire] The Products That Count CPO survey (1,500+ product leaders, published by a product community and a VC, so read skeptically) claims traditional PM headcount is down 30% across industries and up to 70% in SaaS, while a hybrid "product builder" role grew tenfold.[^cpo] And the market is paying for the hybrid: the median advertised salary for forward deployed engineers, the role where an engineer spends most of the week scoping, sitting with users, and building evals rather than writing code, is above $188,000 versus about $145,000 for conventional software engineers.[^fortune-fde]

There is one more piece of evidence I want to include because it is the kind of thing that gets cited without the caveat. Boehm and Basili's 2001 "top ten" list says finding and fixing a defect after delivery is often 100 times more expensive than fixing it during requirements.[^boehm] That number is the standard justification for putting engineers closer to the problem definition. It is also probably wrong as a universal. Menzies and colleagues tested it across 171 projects from 2006 to 2014 and found no consistent delayed-issue effect; their conclusion is that it is a historical relic that shows up in some kinds of projects and not others.[^menzies] I think the honest read is that the cost of a requirements error depends enormously on how cheap it is to rebuild, and cheap rebuilding is exactly what agents give you. Which cuts both ways: it makes getting the spec wrong less catastrophic, and it makes the person who can tell that the spec is wrong more valuable, because now they can just fix it.

So on the second premise, the claim is right, and the evidence is better than the argument usually given for it. The cost of the handoff between "decides what to build" and "builds it" is now higher than the skill gap the handoff was designed to bridge. When building took a quarter, it made sense for a PM to spend weeks getting the spec right first. When a working prototype takes an afternoon, the prototype is the spec, and one person closing the loop beats three people passing documents.

## The obvious objection: why not let the agent do product too?

If agents can write the code, the natural next question is why the engineer should absorb the product work instead of handing that to the agent as well. If the answer is "AI cannot do PM work," the whole argument for engineers moving upstream rests on it, so it is worth stating the argument properly and then saying where it is weak.

The strong form has four parts.

Product work is mostly extracting tacit knowledge from people who have reasons not to give it. Miller's forensic accountants never said they needed to see the math. They said the math was tedious. The real requirement lived in what they did, not what they reported, and surfacing it took someone watching them work and noticing the gap.[^miller] Gartner's Alex Coqueiro names the adversarial version: the domain expert whose job is being automated gives the vendor the official process instead of the real one, and the agent built on it fails on exactly the edge cases they chose not to mention.[^cio-fis] A model works from what it is told. The person who knows the truth often has an incentive not to tell it, and reading that incentive is a social act rather than an information-retrieval one.

Product decisions are bets under accountability, not predictions. Shipping the version that will annoy sales, or killing the feature the CEO likes, is not a question with a correct answer that a better model would find. It is a call someone's name goes on. Cagan's argument that customers are not the source of innovation is the product-side version of this: discovery is not aggregating requests, it is deciding what to believe when the requests conflict, and being the one who is wrong if the bet fails.[^cagan-customers]

The model has no standing in the room. The FDE market pays a premium for what Christian & Timbers calls gravitas, which is a polite word for the ability to make a vice president change their mind.[^ct] Stakeholder work is partly persuasion and partly trust accumulated over time, and neither transfers to a system the stakeholder can ignore without consequence.

And we already have evidence of what happens when the PM loop is automated. Cagan's observation is the datapoint: teams are using AI to read the support queue, generate the roadmap, and write the PRD, and the result is more garbage shipped than at any point in his career.[^cagan] That is not a capability ceiling. It is that the automatable part of PM work was the part that was already producing bad products, and automating it runs the feature factory faster.

Now the weak points, because I do not think this is airtight.

The tacit-knowledge argument is really about access, not cognition. A model that could watch the forensic accountants for a week, with screen recordings and interviews, might well notice they keep scrolling back to check the arithmetic. Nothing in that inference is beyond current models. What is missing is the badge, the trust to be in the room, and the organizational permission to act on what was observed. Those are real barriers, but they are institutional, and institutions move faster than people assume once the economics push.

The accountability argument is the strongest and also says the least about the work. It says a human must sign. It does not say a human must run the interviews, synthesize the findings, or write the options. If 90% of a PM's week is analysis and 10% is signing, "AI cannot do PM work" is true and nearly irrelevant to headcount.

And the "more garbage" observation cuts both ways. It shows that automating a bad process produces bad products. It does not show that AI-assisted good process produces worse products than unassisted good process, and I could not find anyone who has measured that.

Where I land: the part of product work AI genuinely cannot do right now is the part that requires being a person the organization trusts, in a room it lets you into, making a call it will hold you to. That is real, and it is exactly the part engineers absorbing product work are being asked to take on. The part AI can already do is the synthesis and documentation layer, which is most of the visible output of the role and which was, per Cagan, mostly theater at most companies anyway. So the answer to "can AI do PM work" has the same shape as "can AI do engineering": it can do the output, it cannot yet do the ownership, and the ownership was always where the value was. Which is the actual argument for engineers moving upstream. Not that the agent cannot write the PRD, but that someone has to own what the PRD gets wrong, and the person who can also fix it is the cheapest owner.

## Where the claim breaks: the word "every"

This is where I get off the train, and I want to be precise about why, because "not every engineer should do product" sounds like a comfort blanket and I do not mean it as one.

First, the shift is not symmetric, and the framing hides that. The engineer who picks up product judgment keeps their leverage because they can still build. The PM who cannot build is losing leverage. So the actual pressure is "engineers should absorb product judgment," not "the two jobs should merge into one job description." Marina Miller's account of twelve years at Palantir is the best field evidence I have for this: every Echo (the PM-shaped role) was paired with a Delta (the engineer-shaped role), both sat with the user, and the failures she describes came from neither of them watching the user closely enough, not from having two people.[^miller] Her first deployment automated the math a forensic accounting unit did by hand, and nobody used it, because seeing the math was how they verified a case. That lesson required someone in the room, and it did not require the person in the room to be the person who wrote the code.

Second, "talk to stakeholders" and "be a product person" are different sentences, and conflating them is how you get the worst outcome. Nielsen Norman Group's January piece on why empowered teams fail at scale describes the modal PM at a large company spending 70% of their time in coordination: getting buy-in, aligning roadmaps, chasing dependencies, attending working groups.[^nng] That is the stakeholder work. It is not discovery, and Cagan is explicit that customers and stakeholders are not the source of innovation; the team is.[^cagan-customers] If "every engineer should talk to stakeholders" means engineers now attend the alignment meetings, you have taken the part of the PM job that was already failing and distributed it across the people who were the only ones still shipping. The interruption research is unambiguous that this is expensive: practitioners rate planned task switches and meeting-driven switches as about as disruptive as random interruptions, and the main reported consequence is high cognitive cost and low performance.[^switching] Paul Graham's maker's schedule essay is seventeen years old and still the clearest statement of why a single 2pm meeting costs an afternoon.[^pg]

Third, product work is a taste and patience skill, and a lot of excellent engineers do not have it and do not want it. That is not a deficiency. The FDE market is instructive here precisely because it is the extreme case: Christian & Timbers mapped about 17,000 US forward deployed engineers and concluded that around 2,000 have a repeatable track record of turning deployments into measured business value.[^ct] The scarce inputs they name are domain knowledge, credibility in a room with executives, and the ability to build, together. That combination is rare because most people are genuinely better at one side. A team of twelve engineers where all twelve are mandated to be product people is a team where nine are doing something they are mediocre at, and the three who are good at it have no more authority than the nine. Every engineer should be able to run a discovery conversation and write a success metric before building. That is table stakes now, the way reading a profiler became table stakes. Whether someone leans toward the product end or the systems end past that is a specialization, and pretending otherwise wastes the specialists.

Fourth, someone has to own coherence, and "everyone owns product" means nobody does. The NN/g piece's most useful observation is about what happens when many empowered teams each optimize their own metric: a product that looks like it was designed by dozens of companies, because it was.[^nng] Palantir's answer was the Echo. PostHog's answer is small teams with a single accountable engineer per product and a strong written culture. Anthropic's answer is a two-week threshold above which a PM is involved. All three are answers. "Every engineer is a product person" is not an answer; it is the absence of one.

Fifth, and this is the one I think nobody making the claim has priced in: you cannot do discovery without domain, and the industry has stopped hiring the people who would learn it. Big Tech new-grad hiring is down about 65% from 2019.[^signalfire] The Stanford payroll data has employment of 22 to 25 year olds in AI-exposed occupations 19% below trend.[^canaries] Product judgment is built by watching a few hundred users fail at something over years. A team where every engineer is expected to have that judgment from day one is a team that cannot hire anyone under thirty, and that is roughly what the hiring data says the industry has decided to do.

## What history actually says

The split between "decides what to build" and "builds it" is not natural law. It has opened and closed at least three times, and each time the driver was the cost of building.

In the 1960s and 70s the roles were systems analyst and programmer. The analyst talked to the business, wrote the spec, and handed it to the programmer, who translated it into COBOL. The split existed because compute was scarce and programming was slow and specialized enough to be a full-time craft. By the 1980s and 90s the two had mostly merged into "software engineer," partly because tools got better and partly because the handoff was where projects died.

Then the split reopened from the other side. Microsoft created the program manager role in the mid-1980s for the Excel team, explicitly so that someone other than the engineers owned what the product did.[^msft-pm] Google's associate product manager program started in 2002. The 2000s and 2010s produced the modern PM as a standalone career, and the justification was the same every time: engineering is the expensive, slow constraint, so someone else needs to make sure what they build is worth building.

Now the swing is going back, and the driver is the same: building got cheap. PostHog in 2020, Linear, incident.io, and the whole "product engineer" title (searches up 89% since 2021, per PostHog, which has an interest in the term) are the early movers.[^posthog-pe] The FDE, the product builder, and Anthropic's two-week rule are the same movement inside larger organizations.

The pattern: roles split when building is slow and expensive, because the coordination cost of a handoff is worth paying to keep scarce builders building. Roles merge when building is cheap, because the handoff becomes the most expensive step. The 1970s analyst and the 2010s PM are the same role with different job titles, and both existed because of a constraint that has now been relaxed. That does not mean the judgment they carried is obsolete. It means the judgment has to live in a different seat, and the question is which one.

## So should engineers be product people?

Stated as claims you can check against me later.

1. Every engineer should be able to do discovery and scoping without permission, and most cannot today. Sit with the user, write down what they do rather than what they say, define a success metric before building, and make a scoping call. That is the minimum bar now, and the DORA data says teams below it get worse with AI, not better. Anthropic's two-week rule is a reasonable operationalization: below some size, the builder is the PM.

2. The handoff is the waste, not the judgment. The mistake I expect most organizations to make is reading the data as "cut PMs and tell engineers to cover it." That removes the judgment and keeps the process. Cagan's observation that AI makes it easier to run the feature-factory model, faster, with more garbage, is what that looks like in practice. If you remove the PM, you have to also remove the roadmap-of-features and the quarterly commitment theater, or you have just made engineers do two jobs badly.

3. The full hybrid is a specialization, and it should be paid and titled as one. The market already does this: the $188K versus $145K gap, the 2,000-of-17,000 ratio, the "product engineer" and FDE titles. Organizations that mandate everyone become the hybrid will get a regression to the mean. Organizations that identify the people who are actually good at it, give them authority over what gets built, and let the rest go deep on systems will get the leverage.

4. Talking to users is not talking to stakeholders, and the second one is where the time goes to die. The right ask of an engineer is one user conversation a week and a support rotation, which PostHog and others have shown makes better engineers. The wrong ask is a seat in the alignment meeting. If the mandate means the first, agree enthusiastically. If it means the second, ask who is going to do the reviewing that the Vasilescu study says is now the bottleneck.

5. Someone still has to own the whole, and that gets harder as build cost drops. More things shipping means more surface for incoherence. Whether that owner is called PM, Echo, tech lead, or product engineer matters less than that the seat exists and has authority. "Everyone owns it" is how nobody does.

6. The junior problem is the product problem. You cannot mandate product judgment onto people who have never watched a user fail, and the industry has stopped hiring those people. A team that wants engineers with product sense in 2030 has to hire people without it in 2026 and put them in front of users. Very few are doing that, and it will show up before any of the other predictions here do.

What I am not claiming: that the PM role as practiced at most companies survives. Cagan's "product management theater," the person with the title doing business analyst work, was already the majority case and is now the most automatable job in the building. What survives is the judgment, in whichever seat has both the authority and the proximity to the user.

## What I am actually doing about it

I am taking the claim's advice on the part where it is right. One user conversation a week, minimum, on whatever I am building, and I write down what they did rather than what they told me. I have been on the wrong side of this: both OpenSearch incidents I wrote up on this blog were, in hindsight, failures to understand how the people upstream of the cluster were going to load it, and no amount of cluster expertise would have caught that.

I am writing the eval or the success metric before the feature, on anything with a model in it. It is the only artifact from a discovery conversation that survives contact with an engineering team, and it doubles as the review criterion, which is where the actual bottleneck is now.

I am declining the alignment meetings unless I am the one deciding something. That is the PostHog rule and it is also just the maker's schedule. Product proximity means users, not calendars.

And I am pushing back on "every" when it comes up, not because I want to protect engineers from product work, but because the data says the hybrid is rare, valuable, and identifiable, and a mandate is the wrong tool for finding it. Find the people who are already doing it. Give them the authority. Let the rest of us go deep, and put us in front of a user once a week so we remember what deep is for.
